# SOW, SOF, and Sanctions

> **Last reviewed: 2026-04-17.** Source-of-Wealth, Source-of-Funds, and sanctions screening — the three operational disciplines that together do most of the real AML work on a PB book. MAS Notice 626 frames them; this page is the practical RM-level playbook. Interviewers test this area heavily, particularly after the 1MDB episode.

## What each concept is

**Source of Wealth (SOW, 財富來源)** — **how the client's total wealth was accumulated over their lifetime**. The big-picture economic narrative: inherited from family, built from a successful manufacturing business, career earnings as a senior corporate executive, sale of a property portfolio, exit from a technology company, investment gains over decades. SOW is the background story; it answers "how did this person become wealthy."

**Source of Funds (SOF, 資金來源)** — **where this specific inflow or transaction came from**. The immediate trace: "this US$5M came from the sale of Company X completing last month," "this US$2M is a distribution from my family trust Y," "this US$500k is my annual bonus deposited from ABC Bank." SOF answers "where does this money come from, right now."

The two concepts are closely related but distinct and neither substitutes for the other. A rich client with a credible SOW narrative can still have a specific SOF that requires separate documentation — e.g. SOW is "40 years building Asian manufacturing business" and SOF is "US$10M received two weeks ago" — the SOF still needs its own corroboration even though the SOW is established.

## Why they exist — real-world triggers

Virtually every major AML enforcement case turned on deficient SOW and SOF documentation:

- **1MDB (2015–2016)** — funds passed through SG PBs with implausible "general investment" narratives; the PB CDD process treated inbounds with minimal SOF questioning, letting proceeds of fraud flow through.
- **BSI Singapore (2016)** — MAS found "serious lapses at multiple levels" in CDD, including failure to corroborate SOW, weak STR filing, and management failures. Licence revoked.
- **Wells Fargo / Deutsche Russian mirror-trade case (2017)** — mirror trades between Moscow and London branches moved billions in laundered funds; SOF documentation was treated as box-checking.
- **Danske Bank Estonia (2018)** — ~€200B of non-resident flows with patchy KYC over years; shows both the scale and the reporting failure.
- **Credit Suisse Tuna Bonds (2019, Mozambique)** — opaque SOW narratives and conflicts of interest.
- **Various post-2022 sanctions enforcement** — Russian oligarch wealth frequently turned on SOW inadequacy: proceeds of state-linked privatisations, politically-exposed enrichment.

Each case reinforced that SOW / SOF done as paperwork rather than inquiry enables money laundering, and that documented inquiry without corroboration is equally inadequate.

## SOW — the inquiry framework

**Level of due-diligence should match risk.** A well-known corporate executive with publicly-verifiable earnings history doesn't need the same depth as a first-generation wealthy client from a complex-structure family office.

**Core SOW components to establish:**

1. **Career / business narrative** — employment history, business interests, roles held, time periods.
2. **Economic scale check** — does the stated wealth plausibly match the career / business narrative? A 35-year-old claiming US$50M wealth from a 5-year corporate career needs further inquiry.
3. **Timeline coherence** — does the accumulation match plausible earning / asset-growth patterns?
4. **Inheritance / family wealth** — if relevant, documentation of the prior generation's wealth and the inheritance mechanism.
5. **Liquidity events** — IPO proceeds, business sale, property sale, option exercise — with dates and approximate magnitudes.
6. **Investment returns** — historical allocations and rough returns consistent with the current scale.

**Corroboration documents (examples):**

| SOW narrative | Supporting documents |
|---|---|
| Corporate executive / professional career | Employment contracts, compensation statements, tax returns, LinkedIn / public career record |
| Business owner | Corporate registrations, annual financials, tax returns, shareholding documents, news coverage, sale agreements |
| Inheritance | Will / probate, deed of trust, distribution statements, estate tax filings |
| IPO / business sale | Prospectus / IPO allocation, share-sale agreement, escrow documentation |
| Real estate | Sale agreements, property registry records, mortgage history |
| Professional / legal settlement | Settlement agreements, court records |
| Investment gains | Historical brokerage statements, audited portfolio reports |

**Principle:** don't accept narrative alone; don't accept documents alone. Triangulate narrative against documents against public record.

## SOF — the specific-inflow discipline

For every significant inbound into a PB account, SOF documentation is required. "Significant" is typically risk-rated — for high-risk clients, all inbounds; for lower-risk, inbounds above thresholds (e.g. US$500k or a multiple of the account's typical transaction size).

**SOF documentation for common inflows:**

| Inflow type | Typical documentation |
|---|---|
| Business sale proceeds | Signed sale agreement, closing statement, escrow release documentation |
| Dividend / distribution | Dividend notice, corporate-tax filing references |
| Trust distribution | Trust deed, distribution resolution, trustee statement |
| Property sale | Sale contract, closing statement, real-estate agent invoice |
| Bonus / compensation | Employer statement, tax form (W-2, Form 16, P60 equivalent) |
| Inheritance | Probate documentation, estate distribution statement |
| Loan proceeds | Loan agreement, lender identification, purpose statement |
| Investment liquidation | Prior custodian statement, redemption documentation |

**Red-flag SOFs (trigger escalation):**

- Inbound from an unrelated third party with no obvious business relationship.
- Round-number amounts without clear underlying economic rationale.
- Inbound from jurisdictions inconsistent with client's known footprint.
- Inflows that come after outflows of similar size to/from the same or related counterparty ("round-trip").
- Inbounds timed suspiciously around known events (tax-residency changes, regulatory announcements, sanctions dates).
- Client provides a narrative but resists document provision.

**Typology patterns (what launderers try):**

- **Layering** — many small transfers to obscure the origin.
- **Structuring** — amounts just below reporting thresholds.
- **Back-to-back / round-tripping** — funds leave and return through intermediate accounts.
- **Nominee use** — accounts held in a nominee's name where the underlying beneficial owner is sanctioned or higher-risk.
- **Shell-company layering** — funds passing through one or more shell companies with no obvious commercial purpose.
- **Misaligned-narrative laundering** — the "small manufacturing business" paying transfers of unlikely size.

## Politically-exposed persons (PEPs)

A **Politically-Exposed Person (PEP, 政治敏感人物)** is an individual who holds or has held a prominent public function, and therefore poses higher risk of corruption or abuse of office.

**Three PEP categories:**

- **Domestic PEP** — senior public official in the jurisdiction where the account is held (SG PEP for a SG bank).
- **Foreign PEP** — senior public official in any other country.
- **International-organisation PEP** — senior officials of bodies like the UN, World Bank, IMF, WTO, regional development banks.

**Who qualifies** — heads of state / government, senior politicians (including ministers, deputy ministers), senior judges, senior military officers, senior central-bank officials, senior state-owned-enterprise executives, senior political-party officials. Typically extended to **close family members** (parents, spouse, children, siblings) and **close associates** (known business partners, long-term advisors).

**Enhanced due diligence required for PEP accounts:**

- Senior-management approval at account opening.
- Deeper SOW corroboration, focusing on whether the wealth is linked to the public role.
- Ongoing enhanced monitoring.
- Annual review (not deprecated by account age).

**The "former PEP" question.** Most regimes treat former PEPs as still higher-risk for a specified period after leaving office — typically 12–24 months — after which de-categorisation may be considered on a risk-based assessment. The monitoring doesn't just stop on the day they leave office; the family members and business associates often remain PEPs for longer.

## Sanctions screening

**Four sanctions regimes matter for SG banks:**

1. **UN sanctions** — legally binding on all member states, including Singapore.
2. **Singapore sanctions** — Monetary Authority of Singapore targets (a focused list, generally smaller than US or EU).
3. **US OFAC (Office of Foreign Assets Control)** — the most extensive, covering SDN (Specially Designated Nationals), sectoral sanctions programmes, and country sanctions. Applies whenever US-linked infrastructure is touched — USD clearing, US persons, US goods, US technology.
4. **EU and UK sanctions** — similar in structure to OFAC; matter where EU / UK counterparties, infrastructure, or USD-free operations are involved.

**What gets screened:**

- Customer names (and beneficial owners)
- Transactional counterparties (payment senders / recipients)
- Address and identification data
- Related entities and associates
- Ongoing transactional monitoring against updated lists

**Screening tools:** every bank runs commercial screening software (World-Check, LexisNexis, Dow Jones, etc.) against transactions and customers in real-time and ongoing. Hit management: any hit requires investigation to determine whether it's a true match, a false-positive, or a related party. True matches trigger transaction freezing / rejection and further action (reporting, account closure, enforcement cooperation).

**Sanctions-adjacent risks (post-2022 Russia):**

- Family members of sanctioned individuals.
- Entities controlled by sanctioned persons via layered structures.
- Sectoral-sanction exposures (specific industries, not specific persons).
- Indirect exposure through trade routing.

## Red flags / practical application

The full typology list is long; here are the most common RM-observable flags:

| Red flag | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Client resists SOW/SOF documentation | Could indicate the narrative won't hold up |
| Client provides docs that are inconsistent or forged-looking | Forgery of source documents is a crime; report internally |
| Rapid buildup inconsistent with stated career | Scale mismatch classic laundering pattern |
| Narrative-document mismatch | E.g. client says wealth from "technology" but documents show real-estate trading |
| Sudden interest in changing account behaviour | E.g. quiet account suddenly active, or vice versa |
| Pressure to expedite / skip steps | Urgency is a laundering tool |
| Use of nominee or corporate intermediaries without clear reason | Obscures beneficial ownership |
| Adverse media hits | Negative press on the client or close associates |
| Multi-jurisdictional structures without tax / estate rationale | Complexity for complexity's sake |
| Counterparty in sanctioned / high-risk jurisdiction | Even if client passes screening |

### RM playbook — what you actually do

1. **Ask the question directly and non-apologetically.** "Tell me how you built your wealth" is normal onboarding conversation, not an accusation. Clients who've been through PB onboarding before expect it.
2. **Probe beyond the surface.** "Tell me more about…" is your most useful phrase. Specificity pays.
3. **Cross-reference with public record.** A 2-minute Google / LinkedIn / news search often validates or contradicts the narrative.
4. **Document the inquiry, not just the answer.** "I asked X; client said Y; I verified via Z source."
5. **Know when to escalate.** Uncertainty is a signal. Compliance exists to help — not to replace — your judgement.
6. **Tipping-off discipline.** If an STR has been filed, don't hint to the client. See [`mas_notice_626.md`](mas_notice_626.md).
7. **Periodic reviews, not just onboarding.** Risk profile evolves; annual reviews for high-risk, trigger-based for others.

## Interview questions

### Q1. "Client says wealth came from 'family business' — what do you actually ask and document?"

**Answer.** "Family business" alone isn't enough. I'd ask for specifics: What business, what industry, in which countries, what's your role, who else in the family is involved, how long has it been running, what's the revenue / profitability scale, has any part of it been sold. Then I'd ask about his personal role — is he a shareholder, a director, an employee — and how his personal wealth was realised from that business: salary, dividends, share buybacks, sale of shares, inheritance of shares. I'd ask about the non-business components: any investment portfolio he accumulated separately, any real estate holdings, any inheritance he mentioned earlier. Documentation I'd collect: corporate registration or annual financial statements for the business, his shareholding documentation, tax returns for the last 2–3 years showing dividend or salary income consistent with the narrative, any share-sale agreements if he exited any portion, and public news coverage if the business is visible. I'd also cross-reference: search the business name against corporate registries, news, and sanctions. If the narrative matches documents and public record, the SOW is corroborated. If any piece doesn't line up — business too small for the stated wealth, no news coverage for a business he claims is industry-leading, dates inconsistent — I'd flag internally and do deeper inquiry before proceeding. Documentation gets filed as part of the suitability / CDD memo.

### Q2. "What's the difference between SOW and SOF?"

**Answer.** Source of Wealth is the lifetime narrative — how the client became wealthy overall. Career earnings, business building, inheritance, investment returns. Source of Funds is the specific inflow — where this US$5M coming in this week actually came from: a business sale that closed, a dividend distribution, a bonus payment. The two interact but neither substitutes for the other. A client with fully-corroborated SOW (40-year manufacturing career documented with tax returns and corporate records) still needs a specific SOF for a US$3M inbound — "I received a US$3M bonus from my company on [date], here's the statement." The SOF is a specific piece of the SOW puzzle, and missing SOF documentation — even for well-known wealthy clients — is a CDD failure under MAS 626. In practice, SOW is established at onboarding or early relationship; SOF is established at each material transaction going forward.

### Q3. "A client you've known for 5 years is suddenly transferring US$15M in from a new offshore account. Walk me through your process."

**Answer.** Known-for-5-years doesn't buy away the SOF work; if anything it means I have to be extra careful that I'm not giving him benefit of doubt based on comfort rather than facts. Three steps. First, SOF inquiry: ask him directly, non-apologetically, "Tell me about this inflow — where did the funds originate?" Listen. Take note. If the answer is vague, press for specifics. Second, documentation: collect corroboration appropriate to the claimed origin — sale agreement, distribution statement, tax documentation, corporate records. Third, sending-account diligence: what is this offshore account, which institution, is it his, when was it opened, why now — if he's been with us 5 years and suddenly there's an account we don't know about, that's material. Fourth, cross-check: sanctions screening on the originator, adverse-media check, consistency with his known wealth profile — if his known wealth was US$30M and a US$15M inbound appears unexplained, that's a 50% profile expansion that deserves scrutiny. Fifth, internal escalation: this is the scale where I'd brief my team head before processing, and potentially compliance if any flag emerges. Sixth, if clearances pass, I process the inbound with a written memo documenting the SOF narrative, the documents collected, the sanctions / adverse-media checks run, and the internal sign-off. If anything doesn't pass — specifically, if his narrative is shaky, if documents are missing, if the sending account looks off, if sanctions / adverse-media hits emerge — I'd pause and escalate before clearing the transfer. The relationship-duration argument ("but I've known him 5 years") is the wrong frame; the KYC / SOF discipline applies to every material inflow.

### Q4. "Client's nephew is a senior official in a country with corruption concerns. Does that make the client a PEP?"

**Answer.** Not directly, but it does affect the client's risk profile. PEP status extends to "close family members" — typically parents, spouse, children, siblings — so a nephew is generally outside the strict close-family scope. However, two qualifications. First, "close associate" is a separate PEP extension, covering known business partners and close personal associates; if the nephew's senior role means there's significant business entanglement with the client, that could bring the client under the close-associate extension. Second, and more importantly, the nephew's PEP status introduces a **corruption-risk typology flag** even if the client isn't technically a PEP himself — the client's funds could be a vehicle for the nephew's unreported or corrupt wealth, or the client could be receiving funds from the nephew. Practically: I'd treat the client as higher-risk, apply EDD-level SOW inquiry, monitor transactions for flow patterns linked to the nephew's geography or role, and document my rationale. The screening software will likely flag the connection automatically; the bank's PEP framework will give guidance on whether the formal PEP classification applies. I'd work with compliance on the correct classification rather than freelancing — but the risk-based approach should be EDD regardless of formal classification.

### Q5. "What's the practical difference between UN, OFAC, and MAS sanctions for a SG PB?"

**Answer.** All three bind SG-licensed banks, but with different scope and enforcement weight. UN sanctions — legally binding on Singapore as a member state; enforced through SG law; typically narrow targeted lists for terrorism, nuclear proliferation, specific political conflicts. OFAC — US Treasury's sanctions regime; applies *whenever US-linked infrastructure is involved*, which for a PB is essentially always because of USD clearing, US person beneficiaries, US goods / services, or US market access for the bank itself. OFAC has the broadest list by far — SDN list thousands of names, sectoral sanctions programmes for Russia, Iran, North Korea, Venezuela — and the enforcement is severe: fines reach billions, individual banker prosecutions, extraterritorial reach. MAS — Singapore's own targeted sanctions list, narrower than OFAC, focused on SG-specific policy priorities. Practically for the RM: every client screened against all three at onboarding and ongoing; every transaction screened against all three in real-time; hit management escalates to compliance; OFAC is the one where the operational and financial consequences of a breach are largest. Post-2022 Russia sanctions amplified this: OFAC expanded rapidly, including sectoral and related-person sanctions; SG banks had to adjust screening for Russian-connected clients and counterparties, with several client exits required.
