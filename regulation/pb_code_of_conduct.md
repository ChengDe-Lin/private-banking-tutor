# ABS Private Banking Code of Conduct

> **Last reviewed: 2026-04-17.** The industry-led code that governs private-banker conduct in Singapore. Not strictly a MAS regulation, but MAS-supervised and treated as binding in practice. The PB Code is the backstop that translates regulatory principles (suitability, fair dealing, AML) into specific professional-standards expectations you will be measured against on every client interaction.

## What it is

The **Association of Banks in Singapore (ABS) Private Banking Code of Conduct** is the industry-developed and MAS-supervised set of professional and ethical standards for private banking in Singapore. It was first published in 2011 in the wake of post-global-financial-crisis regulatory reform, refined following 1MDB, and updated periodically. The Code is industry-owned — developed by the ABS Private Banking Industry Group (PBIG) — but has regulatory teeth: MAS expects adherence, and the Code is referenced in supervisory examinations.

The Code complements, rather than duplicates, MAS notices. MAS Notice 626 addresses AML/CFT; the SFA addresses capital markets suitability; the Banking Act addresses broader banking conduct. The PB Code sits on top of these and defines the *professional standards and culture* expected in the PB industry specifically.

## Why it exists — the real-world trigger

Three forces shaped the Code:

1. **Post-global-financial-crisis trust deficit (2008–2010).** PB flows dropped as clients questioned whether banks had their interests at heart after the 2008 sell-structured-notes-that-blow-up episodes — e.g. Lehman Mini-Bonds in Singapore that hurt retail-bordered investors. Industry recognised that regulatory rules alone were insufficient to restore trust; a professional-standards framework was needed.
2. **The CACS framework.** The Code was introduced together with the **Client Advisor Competency Standards (CACS)** — the mandatory competency certification for client-facing PB staff in SG. Passing CACS Paper 1 (rules, ethics, compliance) and Paper 2 (products, advice) is the minimum bar; the Code defines what "competency" means in conduct terms.
3. **1MDB and subsequent tightening (2016 onwards).** The code has been referenced heavily in post-1MDB supervisory communications, particularly on cross-border marketing, source-of-wealth documentation, and the "reasonable basis" suitability obligation.

## Who it applies to

- All **ABS-member banks** offering private banking services in Singapore. Effectively all major SG PB operations (UBS, JPM, BOS, Citi, DBS, HSBC, StanChart, Deutsche, BNP, Julius Baer, Pictet, Lombard Odier, LGT, etc.).
- Client-facing private bankers at those institutions — RMs, ICs, product specialists, investment counsellors, portfolio managers in advisory mandates.
- Support staff (compliance, operations, ops-related client-contact roles) to a scaled extent.

Non-ABS-member private bankers (rare in SG) are not directly bound, but MAS supervisory expectations effectively align the whole market to the Code's standards.

## Key obligations — the six pillars

The Code is organised around six pillars. You should be able to name all six, and give a practical example of each.

### 1. Competency

- PB staff must achieve and maintain appropriate technical and product competency.
- **CACS certification** is the minimum regulatory bar — Paper 1 (regulation, conduct, ethics) and Paper 2 (products, investment, advisory). Client-facing staff must pass within a specified period of joining or changing scope.
- Continuing professional development (CPD) — typically 15+ hours/year of structured training.
- Applied specifically: if you're pitching a derivative, you should understand the Greeks at intuition level; if recommending a structured product, know the embedded-option mechanics. "I let the specialist handle that" is a legitimate stance only after you've validated suitability at the RM level.

### 2. Market Conduct

- Fair dealing — no misrepresentation, no misleading inducements.
- Market-manipulation prohibition — no spoofing, layering, pump-and-dump, or coordinated activity distorting prices.
- Integrity in trade execution, best-execution expectations for client orders, fair pricing.
- Information barriers — confidentiality between advisory, proprietary trading, and research; restricted-list / watchlist controls.

### 3. Confidentiality

- Client information is confidential and must be protected from unauthorised disclosure.
- Information cannot be used for personal benefit or for the benefit of other clients.
- Specific sensitivities: cross-border confidentiality (a client in jurisdiction A wants nothing said to authorities in jurisdiction B beyond what law requires), family-member disclosure (spouse, children, adult vs minor), corporate-related non-public information.
- **Balance with AML reporting** — an STR to STRO is a statutory requirement that overrides confidentiality. Tipping-off remains prohibited — see [`mas_notice_626.md`](mas_notice_626.md).

### 4. Cross-Border Marketing

- **This is where many historical enforcement actions concentrate.** Selling PB services cross-border requires adherence to the target jurisdiction's rules — not just SG's.
- Specific restrictions: no marketing to clients in jurisdictions where the bank is not licensed or where the target country's regulator prohibits cross-border solicitation. Physical visits (client-facing bankers travelling to meet clients in the home jurisdiction) are heavily regulated — many countries require licensed visits or prohibit them entirely.
- Examples: strict rules on SEC-registered representatives for US-person clients; PBoC / HKMA / CSRC rules for mainland China engagement; BaFin / FINMA rules for European clients; FSA rules for Japan.
- The "reverse solicitation" defence — client initiates contact, bank responds — has narrow legal basis and is heavily scrutinised post-1MDB.

### 5. AML/KYC

- Aligns with MAS Notice 626 but emphasises the **RM's role as first line of defence**.
- SOW / SOF documentation standards, PEP identification and handling, sanctions screening, ongoing monitoring, STR filing responsibilities.
- Culture of escalation — red flags should be escalated without fear of commercial penalty.

### 6. Transparency (Fees, Conflicts, Recommendations)

- **Fee disclosure** — front-end loads, ongoing management fees, retrocessions / trailer fees, performance fees, structured-product issuance margins. Disclosed in writing; explained in conversation.
- **Conflict disclosure** — when the bank is acting as both issuer and distributor, when trailer fees create steering bias, when proprietary positions overlap with client holdings.
- **Basis for recommendation** — a documented, reasonable basis for each recommendation; suitability record retained.
- **Fair presentation of risk** — risks presented with the same prominence as benefits, not in fine print.

## Relationship to CACS

The CACS (Client Advisor Competency Standards) is the technical competency certification; the PB Code is the conduct / ethics framework. They are complementary:

- CACS Paper 1 covers: SFA, FAA, Banking Act, MAS notices (including 626), the ABS PB Code itself, ethics, and conduct.
- CACS Paper 2 covers: products (equities, FI, FX, derivatives, structured, alternatives), investment theory, portfolio construction, advisory framework.
- The Code is referenced in CACS assessments and its principles are tested.

The path for an SG-licensed PB client-facing role: CACS pass (mandatory for client-facing within 6–12 months of appointment) → ongoing CPD → adherence to Code as ongoing obligation → periodic attestation (annual or on material change).

CACS is separate from international certifications (CFA, CAIA, CMFAS, WMI Certified Private Banker / CPB). Some banks require or encourage additional certifications for Senior RM / Director tracks.

## Red flags / practical application

Situations where the PB Code bites:

- **Cross-border visit without home-jurisdiction licensing** — flying to Taipei / Jakarta / Manila / Mumbai to meet clients without proper licensing or MRA / cooperation arrangements. The post-1MDB enforcement record shows this is live.
- **Fee disclosure gaps** — verbally mentioning a "fee" without specifying trailer split, management fee, and loading; leaving it in small print in onboarding documentation without walking the client through it.
- **Basis-of-recommendation file weakness** — making a suitability call without documenting the reasoning, so that a later review cannot reconstruct "why this product for this client."
- **Specialist hand-off without RM understanding** — introducing a complex structured product via a product specialist without the RM being able to independently explain its mechanics. The PB Code competency pillar bites here.
- **Confidentiality breaches within a family** — telling an adult child about the parent's account without explicit authorisation; sharing across jurisdictions with differing legal privileges.
- **Cross-sell pressure overriding suitability** — AUM targets or product-sales targets creating internal pressure to recommend products that fail suitability. The Code expects escalation, not accommodation.

## Interview questions

### Q1. "Tell me one thing the PB Code says about cross-border marketing."

**Answer.** Cross-border marketing requires adherence to the *target jurisdiction's* rules, not only Singapore's. That means: if I want to market PB services to a client resident in Indonesia, Taiwan, China, or any jurisdiction outside SG, I need to confirm — with compliance — that my bank has the required licences and that my marketing activity complies with that jurisdiction's rules on cross-border solicitation. Many jurisdictions require on-the-ground licensed representation for active solicitation; some prohibit cross-border solicitation entirely and allow only narrow "reverse-solicitation" exceptions where the client themselves initiates the contact. Physical visits by SG-based RMs to meet clients in their home country are heavily regulated — some countries require a local licence, others require the RM to be notified and approved per-trip, others prohibit. Post-1MDB, MAS and peer regulators have tightened enforcement dramatically — there are enforcement cases where SG RMs have been individually penalised for cross-border marketing breaches. Practically: I'd never visit a client in a jurisdiction or solicit a prospect in one without explicit compliance sign-off, and I'd document every cross-border interaction.

### Q2. "What's the difference between the PB Code and MAS Notice 626?"

**Answer.** MAS Notice 626 is a regulatory notice — a legally binding rule issued by MAS under the Banking Act — focused specifically on AML/CFT obligations for banks: customer due diligence, enhanced due diligence, SOW/SOF, ongoing monitoring, STR filing. It's narrow in scope (AML/CFT) but deep, with specific operational requirements and penalty regimes. The PB Code is an industry-developed code owned by the Association of Banks in Singapore PB industry group, covering the broader conduct and professional-standards landscape in private banking specifically — competency, market conduct, confidentiality, cross-border marketing, AML/KYC, and transparency. The PB Code is broader (six pillars vs AML focus) and more principles-based; it's not legally binding in the same way as a MAS notice, but MAS supervises industry adherence and uses it in examinations. Practically the two work together: Notice 626 tells me *what* AML steps I must do; the PB Code tells me *how* I should conduct myself across the full relationship, with AML being one of six pillars. An enforcement action could cite both, and post-1MDB many have.

### Q3. "How would you handle a case where your AUM target is pressuring you toward a recommendation that feels borderline on suitability?"

**Answer.** I'd separate the two questions completely. First, the suitability question is binary: either this product is suitable for this client, or it is not. That question isn't affected by my AUM target. If it's not suitable, I don't recommend it — regardless of the pressure. Second, the commercial question — how do I hit my target — is my problem, not the client's. The answer is finding genuinely suitable flow from this client or other clients. If I'm feeling pressure to stretch suitability, I'd escalate to my team head or manager directly: "I'm running short on Q-end AUM and I want to check whether there are legitimate opportunities I'm missing, rather than stretching suitability on marginal cases." Most senior RM leaders will respect that framing; in the rare case they push back ("just push the product"), that itself is a compliance-signal I'd raise higher. The PB Code explicitly expects escalation rather than accommodation of suitability stretch. Beyond the individual case — a sustained pattern of "AUM pressure → suitability stretch" is the posture that generates the enforcement cases I want to avoid. I'd rather miss a quarter's target than be in a complaints queue in 18 months.

### Q4. "Explain fee disclosure to a client as the PB Code would expect."

**Answer.** I'd walk through four fee components explicitly: upfront / front-end load (if applicable), ongoing management fee (expressed as % of NAV/yr), retrocession or trailer fee flowing from the fund manager to our bank as distributor (% of fund NAV/yr, typically 30–50% of the management fee), and — if a structured product — the issuance margin or arranger fee built into the initial product price. For a hypothetical mutual fund: "This fund has a 2% front-end load, which I can often negotiate down for a ticket your size. The ongoing management fee is 1.5% per year, of which roughly 0.5% flows to our bank as a trailer fee for recommending and servicing the fund. There's no performance fee on this class. So your net gross drag from fees is about 1.5% per year if I negotiate the load; more if we don't. For the same strategy, a clean share class in a DPM mandate would charge 1.5% directly to the manager with no trailer, and the bank would earn the separate DPM management fee on total AUM. I want you to see both structures before we decide." That disclosure pattern — specific numbers, clear conflict flagging, alternative framing offered — is what the Code expects. Most clients appreciate the transparency; a few are surprised; none complain.

### Q5. "A client in a sanctions-adjacent country asks to maintain confidentiality of his SG account. What does the Code say?"

**Answer.** The Code's confidentiality obligation is strong but not absolute — it operates within the law. Specifically: client confidentiality does not override statutory reporting obligations (STR filing under CDSA, tax reporting under FATCA/CRS, court orders with jurisdiction). The Code also doesn't override sanctions screening — if a client or their counterparty is on a sanctions list, the bank must act regardless of confidentiality preference. For the specific scenario: if the client is from a sanctions-adjacent jurisdiction — meaning the country itself is not sanctioned but has sanctioned individuals, or has geopolitical sensitivity — I'd treat the confidentiality request as natural, consistent with standard PB confidentiality, while being clear in the suitability file that: (a) the client is in or associated with that jurisdiction, (b) we've done sanctions screening of the client and key associates, (c) we've documented SOW/SOF with appropriate corroboration, and (d) we've flagged elevated AML risk in his profile. The confidentiality expected under the Code is confidentiality from improper disclosure — not confidentiality from legal obligations or from internal risk controls. I'd be careful to explain to the client that the confidentiality he's asking for is exactly what we already provide as standard; if he's asking for something beyond that — concealing beneficial ownership, suppressing tax reporting, declining sanctions screening — that's not something we can or will do, and the conversation immediately goes to compliance.
