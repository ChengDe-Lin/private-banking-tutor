# Accredited Investor (AI) and Expert Investor (EI)

> **Last reviewed: 2026-04-17.** The classification that gates most of the PB product shelf in Singapore. Thresholds and the opt-in regime are load-bearing. This page covers the current regime; exact numbers are stable but always worth reconfirming before the interview.

## What it is

Under the **Singapore Securities and Futures Act (SFA)**, individual and corporate investors are classified into tiers based on their sophistication and financial position. Two tiers most relevant for PB:

- **Accredited Investor (AI, 合格投資者)** — an investor meeting specified financial thresholds who is presumed capable of understanding and bearing higher-risk investment products. Forms the overwhelming majority of PB client base in SG.
- **Expert Investor (EI, 專業投資者)** — a narrower category for investors with specialised financial knowledge or institutional roles (licensed financial professionals, corporate treasurers, etc.).

AI / EI classification determines what products a client can be offered under SFA **exemptions** — specifically, products that would otherwise require a full prospectus and retail suitability safeguards can be offered to AIs / EIs without those requirements.

## Why it exists

The SFA is premised on the idea that retail investors need substantial protection — prospectus disclosure, product-highlights-sheets, suitability frameworks, cooling-off periods, advertising restrictions — but **sophisticated investors can navigate more complex products with less hand-holding**. The AI / EI framework formalises this: a class of investors presumed able to evaluate complex products on their own informed basis, in exchange for waiving some of the retail protections.

Without the AI / EI exemption, structured products, hedge funds, most private-equity vehicles, non-UCITS alternatives, and many derivatives simply couldn't be distributed in SG at all — the prospectus burden would make them uneconomic. The regime is what makes the PB industry operationally viable.

**Why the opt-in regime came in (2019).** Until 2019, classification was automatic — if you met the thresholds, you were an AI by default. After MAS consultation and industry feedback following 1MDB and other episodes, MAS shifted to an **opt-in regime**: even if you meet the thresholds, you must affirmatively opt in before the bank can treat you as an AI. This ensures the client consciously chooses the lighter-protection regime rather than defaulting into it. The bank must provide clear disclosure of what protections are being waived.

## Who it applies to

Any natural person or corporation that meets the threshold tests and has opted in.

### AI thresholds (individual) — meet ANY ONE of three tests

Current thresholds (stable since 2019 with possible inflation adjustments — verify before interview):

| Test | Threshold | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Net personal assets** | > S$2 million | Primary residence capped at S$1M contribution (to prevent an otherwise-modest individual with a valuable home qualifying purely on property) |
| **Financial assets (excluding primary residence)** | > S$1 million | Specifically financial assets — cash, investments, deposits; excludes real estate, business interests, personal property |
| **Income in preceding 12 months** | > S$300,000 | Stable employment income or business income; one-off bonuses / sale proceeds not counted |

**Individual: meet any one and you qualify for AI status (subject to opt-in).**

### AI thresholds (corporate)

- Net assets > S$10 million (corporate entity) — OR
- All beneficial owners are themselves accredited investors.

Used for family investment companies, private investment companies, and corporate-vehicle structures that UHNW clients commonly employ.

### EI thresholds

Narrower. Individual EIs include:
- CMS-licensed financial professionals (fund managers, traders) with certain years of experience;
- Officers of financial institutions with specified roles;
- Certain other designated categories.

EI status provides even wider exemptions than AI, but the category covers relatively few people.

### The opt-in mechanics

Post-2019, the process is:

1. Bank assesses the client's financial position via documentation (net worth statement, bank statements, income documentation).
2. If the client meets any of the thresholds, the bank presents an **AI opt-in disclosure** clearly explaining what protections are being waived by opting in (detailed below).
3. Client signs the opt-in acknowledgement.
4. Bank is now permitted to treat the client as AI — applying AI-only product exemptions going forward.
5. Opt-in is **periodically re-confirmed** (typically annually or trigger-based) to ensure the client still meets thresholds and still consciously chooses AI status.
6. Client can **opt out** at any time, reverting to retail-investor treatment for future transactions.

## Key obligations (for the bank, regarding AI clients)

- **Threshold verification** — document and retain evidence that the client meets thresholds at onboarding and at opt-in.
- **Opt-in disclosure** — provide clear, written disclosure of what protections are being waived. The disclosure is specified in MAS guidance.
- **Opt-in acknowledgement** — retain signed acknowledgement.
- **Periodic re-verification** — confirm the client still meets thresholds and still opts in, typically annually.
- **Right to opt out** — the client must be able to opt out unilaterally and the bank must process the opt-out promptly.
- **Product shelf discipline** — ensure AI-only products are only offered to AI-status clients; retail products remain available to all.
- **Suitability still applies** — AI status waives prospectus / marketing protections but does NOT waive suitability. The bank still has a reasonable-basis-for-recommendation obligation under the SFA Code of Conduct and the PB Code; AI status doesn't mean "anything goes."

### What protections are waived when a client opts in to AI?

This is the key interview point. Opt-in is *consent to waive retail-investor protections*, specifically:

- **No prospectus / simplified offering document** — AI-only products don't require the detailed prospectus that retail products need under the SFA.
- **No Product Highlights Sheet (PHS)** — the standard retail disclosure document isn't required.
- **No advertising restrictions** — AI-only products can be privately marketed; retail advertising rules don't apply.
- **Shorter or no cooling-off periods** — retail products often have a cancellation window after purchase; AI products may not.
- **Lighter suitability framework** — note the qualifier "lighter" not "none." The CAD (Customer's Account Documentation) requirements for AI are reduced but not zero.
- **Access to wider product universe** — structured products with higher risk, hedge funds, PE feeder funds, unrated bonds, HY bonds, derivatives with leverage — all become accessible.

### What does NOT change with AI status

- The bank's duty of care, honest dealing, and avoidance of misrepresentation remain.
- AML/CFT obligations remain (MAS Notice 626 is orthogonal to AI classification).
- FATCA / CRS reporting obligations remain.
- The ABS PB Code's conduct expectations remain in full.
- Client's legal rights under CDSA, the Consumer Protection Act, etc., remain.

## Red flags / practical application

Situations where AI / EI classification matters in practice:

- **Client wants to buy a structured note but hasn't opted in.** The bank cannot sell. Fix: have the threshold-verification and opt-in done before offering the product.
- **Client opted in but thresholds no longer met.** E.g. a client had S$1.5M in financial assets, drew down to S$800k, still signed the annual opt-in. On next review, threshold miss. Bank must revert to retail treatment or find another threshold the client meets.
- **Trust / family investment company structures.** Who's the "investor" for AI purposes — the trustee, the settlor, the beneficiary, the underlying company? Usually the entity itself is assessed (corporate AI thresholds), but layered structures require legal review.
- **Joint accounts** — both holders typically must meet AI thresholds or both opt in. Specifics depend on the account arrangement.
- **Minor children's accounts** — typically cannot be AI; held in trust or under parental guardianship with retail protections.
- **Non-resident clients** — AI classification is a SG legal construct. Overseas clients being booked in SG follow the SG rules, but their home-jurisdiction rules may have additional classification regimes (Reg D / QIB in the US, MiFID Professional/Elective Professional in EU, etc.). Multi-jurisdictional clients often have different classification across books.

## Interview questions

### Q1. "A client has S$2.5 million of financial assets but hasn't opted in. Can you sell him a structured note?"

**Answer.** No — not directly. The 2019 opt-in regime changed AI status from automatic to opt-in. Even though he meets the financial-assets threshold (>S$1M), he has not affirmatively opted in, so he is still treated as a retail investor. Structured notes that are AI-exempt-only cannot be sold to him. Fix: walk him through the AI opt-in disclosure, explain what protections he's waiving — prospectus, PHS, advertising restrictions, cooling-off periods — ensure he understands, obtain the opt-in acknowledgement, and then proceed. I wouldn't do the opt-in and the product pitch in the same meeting — that creates a "pressure opt-in" feel; I'd separate them. Do the opt-in as a standalone conversation framed as "unlocks the full PB product shelf"; the product pitch comes next meeting as normal. Documentation needs to show (a) threshold verification, (b) written opt-in, and (c) separate written suitability file for the specific product — all three.

### Q2. "What protections does a client waive by opting in to AI status?"

**Answer.** Five main ones. First, the product doesn't need a full SFA prospectus — issuers can offer AI-only products under an exemption, with much thinner disclosure documentation. Second, no Product Highlights Sheet — the standardised retail disclosure is not required. Third, retail advertising restrictions don't apply — the products can be privately marketed with content that would be prohibited in a retail context. Fourth, statutory cooling-off periods may not apply to AI-only products. Fifth, certain lighter suitability-framework requirements apply rather than the full retail flow. What doesn't change: the bank's duty of care, honest dealing, reasonable-basis-for-recommendation obligation under the SFA Code of Conduct and ABS PB Code, and all AML, sanctions, FATCA/CRS obligations. AI status unlocks the product universe; it doesn't turn off the bank's conduct obligations.

### Q3. "A long-term client's assets have declined below the threshold due to market losses and divorce settlement. What do you do?"

**Answer.** Three steps. First, verify current financial position — updated documentation of net worth, financial assets, recent income. If the client still meets any of the three AI tests (net personal assets > S$2M, financial assets > S$1M, or income > S$300k/yr), re-opt-in at the annual review and continue. If he fails all three, he's reverted to retail status for future transactions. Second, walk him through the implications: he can continue to hold existing AI-only positions; they aren't forced-unwound. But any *new* AI-only product recommendations are off-limit until threshold is re-met. For ongoing portfolio management, we'd shift toward retail-available products — still plenty of depth, but certain structured products, specific HF and PE funds, and certain bond issues are unavailable. Third, if the shortfall is temporary — divorce settlement is completing in 3 months and he'll be back above thresholds — I'd document that timing expectation and plan the transition. If permanent, this is a frank conversation about a different product shelf going forward. Practically, his relationship with the bank continues; the product-access universe narrows. Handling it cleanly, honestly, and without attempting workarounds is the Code posture.

### Q4. "Why did MAS move from automatic to opt-in for AI in 2019?"

**Answer.** Two main drivers. First, post-1MDB scrutiny revealed that many clients were being treated as AI by default without really understanding what protections they were waiving — classification happened as a quiet piece of onboarding paperwork rather than as an informed choice. Second, industry consultation found that AI classification had become a blanket default for anyone with moderate wealth, including clients who might have benefited from retail protections for specific product types. The opt-in regime forces a conscious client choice: "yes, I know what I'm waiving, and yes, I want the broader product access." It also creates an annual re-confirmation check, so transient or misunderstood opt-ins get revisited. Third-order benefit: the regime pushed banks to improve the quality of the AI opt-in disclosure and the conversation with clients about what they're actually consenting to. The net effect is a slower onboarding in some cases but a cleaner regulatory posture and better-documented client intent.

### Q5. "What's the difference between AI and Expert Investor — when does EI matter in PB?"

**Answer.** AI is defined by financial thresholds — net worth, financial assets, or income — and presumes wealth-based sophistication. EI is defined by role or professional qualification — CMS-licensed financial professionals with certain years of experience, corporate treasurers of specified types, certain institutional officers. EI implies actual technical sophistication, not just wealth. In practice, EI is much narrower than AI and covers relatively few people. When it matters for PB: (a) when a client is a licensed financial professional (fund manager, trader, senior bank staff), they often qualify as EI directly, not just AI, which sometimes allows even wider product access; (b) for institutional counterparties in a mixed-client book where the institution has specified EI-eligible staff; (c) very rarely for UHNW family-office staff with specific licensed roles. For most PB clients — successful business-people, professionals, HNW families — AI is the relevant category, and EI is a niche additional tier. The distinction matters in documentation and product-tier eligibility but not in the day-to-day RM conversation.
