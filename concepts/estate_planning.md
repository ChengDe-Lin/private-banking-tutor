# Estate Planning, Trusts, and Family Governance

> **For UHNW and family-office clients, this is the conversation that defines the multi-decade relationship.** Portfolio returns matter; estate structures decide whether the wealth actually transfers intact to the next generation. Interview questions typically blend trust mechanics + family-governance judgement + cross-border tax awareness. Candidates from finance backgrounds who haven't been exposed to this layer often under-answer it badly.

## 直覺理解

Estate planning (遺產規劃) 不是「填一份遺囑」的行政動作——是**為「死亡、失能、家族爭執、跨境、稅務」這五個情境設計提前防禦**。對 UHNW 客戶而言，這層比 portfolio 重要得多：你選錯基金只是少賺幾個百分點，沒處理好 estate 可能讓家族花三代打官司。核心工具是**信託 (trust)**——法律上把資產從你名下切到「信託」名下，由 trustee 按 settlor 寫的規則發給受益人 (beneficiaries)。信託解決的三件事：**隔離** (asset protection 防債務 / 離婚分割)、**延續** (死後仍依意志運作)、**跨境稅務優化**。PPLI (Private Placement Life Insurance) 是信託的變形包裝，特別適合有 US-person 繼承人的亞洲家族。**你不需要會寫信託契約**——那是律師的工作——但你必須**說得出什麼情境該觸發 trust 對話，哪種結構大概用在哪種客戶**。

## Why estate planning matters — the five scenarios

Every UHNW client's estate structure must answer these five questions before it's complete.

| Scenario | Without planning | With proper planning |
|---|---|---|
| **Death of primary holder** | Assets frozen pending probate (months to years); family liquidity stressed; tax liabilities bunch | Trust / insurance bypasses probate; liquidity continuous; tax pre-optimised |
| **Incapacity / cognitive decline** | Assets unreachable without court-appointed guardian; decisions delayed; family fights | Lasting power of attorney; successor-trustee activates smoothly |
| **Family conflict / divorce** | Spouse may claim 50%+; children litigate; public family disputes | Assets in trust often protected from divorce / creditor claims |
| **Cross-border exposure** | Multiple jurisdictions claim estate tax simultaneously; assets stranded | Situs planning + trust + treaty optimisation minimises double-tax |
| **Generation-skipping** | Tax hits at each generational transfer | Dynasty trust / generation-skipping transfer structures defer |

If you can't articulate these five scenarios in a client meeting, the client doesn't know why the conversation matters.

## The basic documents every HNW+ client should have

### 1. Will

- Directs the distribution of probate assets (assets in your own name, not in trust).
- Names executor(s), guardian(s) for minor children, specific bequests.
- **Jurisdiction matters.** Wills drafted under SG law, HK law, TW law each have specific requirements. A HK will on SG assets may need resealing in SG or a separate SG-law will.
- **Multi-jurisdictional approach:** for clients with assets in 3+ jurisdictions, typically one "primary" will per jurisdiction that has probatable assets, carefully drafted so they don't contradict each other.

### 2. Lasting Power of Attorney (LPA, in SG) / Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA, in HK)

- Designates who can make decisions on your behalf if you become mentally incapacitated.
- Two varieties: **personal welfare** (medical, living arrangements) and **property and affairs** (financial, legal).
- Must be prepared and certified while capacity is intact.

### 3. Advance Medical Directive (AMD) / Living Will

- Medical wishes if incapacitated — life-support preferences, organ donation, pain management.
- Separate from LPA; operates in parallel.

### 4. Letter of wishes

- **Non-binding** guidance to trustees, executors, and family.
- Specifies intent where legal documents are general: which child gets the business, which the property; philanthropic priorities; family values you want preserved.
- Can be updated freely without triggering legal review.

**These four documents are the minimum viable estate stack.** Any UHNW client without all four has a gap.

## Trusts — the core structure

### What a trust is (legal reality)

A **trust** (信託) is a legal relationship where a **settlor** (設立人, also called grantor / trustor) transfers ownership of assets to a **trustee** (受託人), who holds and manages them according to the trust deed for the benefit of **beneficiaries** (受益人).

Key mental model:

```
Settlor  ──(transfers assets)──▶  Trust (legal entity)
                                   │
                                   ├── Trustee: manages per deed
                                   ├── Protector (optional): oversight, can replace trustee
                                   └── Beneficiaries: receive income / principal per deed
```

Once assets are in the trust, the settlor **generally no longer owns them** — the trust is a separate legal structure with its own tax identity. This separation is what gives the trust its asset-protection and tax properties.

### Key trust concepts

| Term | What it means | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| **Revocable vs Irrevocable** | Revocable: settlor can amend/cancel. Irrevocable: cannot (or only with beneficiary consent). | Only irrevocable provides full asset-protection / tax-separation benefits |
| **Discretionary vs Fixed** | Discretionary: trustee decides distributions. Fixed: deed specifies. | Discretionary offers more flexibility and stronger creditor/divorce protection |
| **Onshore vs Offshore** | SG-law trust vs Jersey/Guernsey/Cayman/BVI trust | Offshore: stronger asset-protection statutes, privacy, but more reputation risk post-CRS |
| **Grantor vs Non-Grantor** (US tax term) | Grantor: settlor still taxed on income. Non-Grantor: trust itself taxed. | Critical for US-person clients |
| **Protector** | Third party with override powers (replace trustee, consent to distributions) | Adds governance layer; family-senior often serves |
| **Letter of wishes** | Non-binding guidance to trustee | Preserves settlor's intent without legal rigidity |

### Common trust types a PB client may use

1. **Family trust (revocable)**
   - Simple; settlor retains control; used for probate avoidance and privacy.
   - Does NOT protect from settlor's creditors or divorce.
   - Entry-level structure for HNW clients who want to avoid probate but aren't yet ready for full asset-separation.

2. **Irrevocable discretionary trust**
   - Settlor irrevocably transfers assets; trustee has discretion over distributions.
   - Full asset-protection (from settlor's later creditors/divorce).
   - Common for UHNW who've completed wealth-accumulation phase.

3. **Dynasty trust / Generation-skipping trust**
   - Long-duration (often perpetual in some jurisdictions) trust benefiting multiple generations.
   - Defers estate / transfer tax across generations.
   - Common UHNW structure for multi-generational wealth.

4. **Insurance trust (ILIT — Irrevocable Life Insurance Trust)**
   - Holds life insurance policies outside the settlor's estate.
   - Proceeds bypass estate tax; used heavily for US-person liquidity planning.

5. **Charitable trust / Charitable remainder trust**
   - Donates to charity with income stream to beneficiaries or vice versa.
   - Tax-advantaged philanthropy vehicle.

6. **Asset-protection trust**
   - Specifically designed to shield from creditors; typically offshore (Cook Islands, Nevis).
   - Reputation risk: may draw scrutiny in litigation; choose jurisdiction carefully.

### Onshore vs offshore — the choice

**SG-law trust:**
- Singapore Trustees Act; well-developed legal framework.
- Reputation clean; fully within SG regulatory and tax system.
- Potentially taxable in SG if trust income isn't distributed (consult tax counsel).
- Growing in popularity, especially with the MAS 13O/13U family-office regime.

**Jersey / Guernsey trusts:**
- Top-tier reputation for wealth structures; widely used for UHNW.
- Strong legal framework, professional trustee industry, tax-neutral.
- Higher cost; CRS-reporting compliant; EU-regulatory footprint can matter post-Brexit.

**BVI / Cayman / Bahamas trusts:**
- Tax-neutral, private, flexible.
- Some jurisdictions have reputation issues; CRS participation varies.
- Still very common for Asian UHNW with decades-established relationships.

**Cook Islands / Nevis trusts:**
- Strongest asset-protection statutes (designed specifically to defeat foreign judgments).
- Reputation risk — can look like concealment in litigation.
- Rarely default recommendation; specific use cases only.

### Trust failure modes

- **Settlor over-retention of control** — if the settlor essentially continues to control the assets (as grantor-trustee or via puppet trustee), courts may treat the trust as a "sham" and unwind it. Common in poorly-drafted early trusts.
- **Gift-tax consequences** — in jurisdictions with gift/inheritance tax, transferring assets to an irrevocable trust can itself be a taxable gift. Often requires careful timing or use of exemptions.
- **CRS reporting complexity** — trust structures must be reported; beneficial ownership of trusts is subject to transparency rules.
- **US-person beneficiary problem** — if any beneficiary is a US person, the trust may trigger complex US tax reporting (throwback rules, foreign grantor/non-grantor classification). Requires specialist US counsel, not bank counsel.

## PPLI — Private Placement Life Insurance

**PPLI is a life insurance contract used as a wealth-structuring wrapper.** A favourite tool for HNW / UHNW clients with US-person family members, cross-border complexity, or specific tax-efficiency goals.

### Mechanics

1. Client (or their trust) pays a substantial premium into a life insurance policy issued by a specialised carrier (Bermuda, Luxembourg, Liechtenstein, Singapore).
2. The premium funds an **investment account inside the policy** — typically a separate account managed per the client's mandate (bespoke, often via the PB's investment team).
3. The investment grows tax-deferred within the policy; gains and income are not taxed to the owner (depending on jurisdiction) while in the policy.
4. On death, the beneficiary receives the policy's full value **as an insurance death benefit**, often free of income tax and sometimes outside the estate for estate-tax purposes.

### Why PPLI appeals

- **Tax deferral** — investment growth compounds pre-tax within the policy.
- **Beneficiary protection** — death benefit bypasses probate; often outside estate tax.
- **US-person efficiency** — legitimately converts taxable investment income into non-taxable death proceeds for US beneficiaries.
- **Customised investment mandate** — unlike retail life insurance, PPLI allows client-specific investment strategies.
- **Cross-border portability** — carriers in neutral jurisdictions (Bermuda, Luxembourg) serve multi-jurisdictional clients.
- **Asset protection** — in some jurisdictions, life insurance is creditor-protected.

### Downsides and complexity

- **Minimum premium** — usually US$2M–$5M+; not for HNW with moderate liquidity.
- **Fee layers** — policy fees (0.5–1.5%/yr), investment management fees, M&E (mortality and expense) charges. All-in costs often 100–200 bps/yr on top of investment fees.
- **Lock-up** — partial withdrawals possible but full redemption (surrender) has penalties and tax consequences.
- **Regulatory complexity** — policy must meet jurisdiction-specific rules to maintain its tax-advantaged character (e.g. US diversification rules under IRC §817).
- **Insurance-law governance** — you're now dealing with an insurance contract, not a pure investment. Requires specialist advisors.
- **Underwriting** — client must be insurable; health-based underwriting required.

### When to recommend PPLI

- UHNW client with US-person beneficiaries and long-horizon tax deferral goals.
- Cross-border family needing a single structure that works across multiple jurisdictions.
- Clients seeking asset protection plus tax efficiency in combination.
- NOT recommended for clients under US$2M in liquid assets, short-horizon clients, or those uncomfortable with long-term lock-up.

### Common PPLI variants

- **Bermuda PPLI** — most common for Asian UHNW; clean reputation.
- **Luxembourg PPLI** — EU-regulated; common for European clients.
- **Singapore-issued PPLI** — MAS-regulated insurers; growing; attractive for SG-resident clients.
- **US-compliant PPLI** — specifically designed for US-person owners; meets IRC §817(h) diversification rules.

## Family governance — beyond the legal structures

Trusts and wills solve legal continuity; **family governance** solves the human dynamics of multi-generational wealth. Without family governance, even the best-drafted structures produce fighting cousins.

### The family charter / constitution

A family charter is a **written document expressing the family's shared values, principles, and decision-making framework around wealth.** It is not legally binding but serves as the moral / cultural constitution.

Typical contents:
- Family values and purpose of the wealth.
- Who is considered family (spouses? in-laws? half-siblings?).
- Decision-making bodies: family council, next-gen forum, family assembly.
- Rules for family-member employment in family businesses.
- Rules for capital requests (loans, distributions, startup funding for next-gen).
- Philanthropy framework.
- Conflict-resolution procedures.
- Education and mentoring commitments for next-gen.

### Family council

The **family council** is the governance body (typically 5–12 people representing branches / generations) that meets 2–4 times a year to:
- Review family-wealth performance and family-office operations.
- Make decisions on family-level issues (new members joining, capital requests, philanthropy priorities).
- Approve family charter amendments.
- Nurture relationships across branches.

### Next-generation development

One of the most commonly overlooked items — the next generation is often financially educated too late:
- **Structured education program** — financial literacy, investment basics, the family business story.
- **Mentoring assignments** — junior family members shadow advisors and trustees.
- **Paid internships** in the family office or portfolio companies (with performance expectations, not sinecures).
- **Capital "training wheels"** — a small investment account managed by the next-gen member with real accountability.

### Conflict patterns the best governance anticipates

- **Active vs passive family members** — some run the business, others receive distributions; resentment builds.
- **Spouses** — in-laws often precipitate disputes; charter should address their standing clearly.
- **Generational values mismatch** — founder's risk tolerance ≠ grandchildren's ESG priorities.
- **Liquidity demands** — one branch wants income, another wants growth; structure must handle both.
- **Concentration events** — crises force decisions; having governance in place beforehand saves relationships.

## SFO vs MFO — the family-office decision

### Single-Family Office (SFO)

- Dedicated team serving ONE family.
- Total control; full customisation; full cost borne by family.
- Typically justified at **US$100M+ AUM** (cost vs benefit below that is hard to justify).
- SG-resident families increasingly use this model post-MAS 13O tax-incentive regime.
- Full-scope SFO headcount: 5–15+ (CEO / CIO / CFO / CAO / legal / tax / admin / junior analysts).
- Annual operating cost: US$2–5M+ for a well-run SFO.

### Multi-Family Office (MFO)

- Shared team serving multiple families (typically 10–100 families).
- Access to dedicated expertise at lower per-family cost.
- Reduced customisation; some governance is shared infrastructure.
- Viable at **US$10M–100M AUM** — the sweet spot where full SFO is unjustified but bank-PB is insufficient.
- MFO tier fees typically 50–100 bps/yr on AUM plus some performance fees on alpha.

### The Bank PB vs FO distinction

- **Bank PB** — client is a customer of the bank; bank's product shelf; revenue from transactions + AUM + NIM + retrocessions.
- **MFO** — client is a member of the office; curated platform sourcing from multiple banks + direct investments; revenue purely on AUM.
- **SFO** — client IS the office; everything custom; revenue none (family funds operation).

Many wealthy families use a **combination**: FO for strategic asset allocation + direct investments, bank PB for execution + specific products + banking services. The interview-relevant framing is understanding when each fits.

### Singapore 13O / 13U incentive — the reason SG is booming for family offices

MAS Section 13O (fund manager) and 13U (offshore fund) tax-incentive schemes provide tax exemption on specified investment income for qualifying family-office structures. Key requirements (as of 2024–2025 — verify current before interview):

- **13O**: min AUM S$20M; min 2 investment professionals; min S$200k local expenses.
- **13U**: min AUM S$50M (for one-family); min 3 investment professionals (at least 1 non-related); min S$500k local expenses.
- Tighter rules after 2022 to improve quality of applicants.
- Must hold designated investments (bonds, equities, funds, etc.) — excludes most direct real estate and most private-company equity.
- Certified Family Office status grants ongoing tax exemption.

This is the regime behind the post-2020 family-office migration from HK to SG. Interview answer for "why SG for family offices": not just political stability and rule of law — this specific tax framework is the structural driver.

## Cross-border estate — the US reach

### Why US estate tax matters even for non-US clients

The US imposes **estate tax on US-situs assets** of any owner, regardless of the owner's citizenship or residency.

**US-situs assets include:**
- US real estate (FIRPTA rules).
- Shares of US corporations (yes, just owning Apple stock counts).
- US bonds (some exceptions — portfolio interest generally not US-situs).
- Cash in US bank accounts (generally NOT US-situs for estate-tax purposes for non-US-persons).
- Tangible property physically in the US.

**Non-US persons** face US estate tax on US-situs assets above a very low threshold (US$60,000 lifetime exemption) at rates up to **40%**. A non-US HNW client holding US$5M of Apple stock in their personal name who dies has a US estate-tax exposure of roughly US$2M.

### Common mitigations

- **Hold US-situs assets through a non-US corporation** (e.g. a BVI company) — the shares of the non-US corporation are non-US-situs; the US-situs assets are owned by the corporation, not the individual.
- **Hold through a non-US trust** — trust assets are not in the settlor's estate.
- **Life insurance / PPLI** — death proceeds often outside estate.
- **US estate-tax treaty** — some jurisdictions (UK, Australia, some EU) have treaties reducing double-tax; many Asian jurisdictions do not.
- **Gift pre-death** — transfer US-situs assets to heirs before death; consult gift-tax rules.

### US person beneficiary problem

Even if the settlor is non-US, if any **beneficiary is a US person**, the trust triggers:
- Throwback rules (accumulated income taxed on distribution).
- FATCA reporting to IRS.
- PFIC rules on underlying investments.
- FBAR / Form 8938 disclosure on the beneficiary.

Specialist US tax counsel required. Do NOT freelance.

## Interview questions you should be able to answer

### Q1. "A client has US$50M globally, wants to pass wealth to three children without family conflict. Where do you start?"

**Answer.** Not with structures — with a conversation. I'd propose a 2-hour working session with the client and spouse (if applicable) covering five questions: one, what's the family situation — ages, capabilities, values of each child; two, what's the wealth picture by jurisdiction, type, liquidity; three, what outcome they actually want — equal distribution, merit-based, needs-based, staged; four, timing preference — during lifetime via gifts, at death via trust/will, mixed; five, governance preference — do they want to stay involved, delegate to a trustee, involve children in decisions. From that conversation, I'd sketch a provisional structure: typically a combination of lifetime gifts (exhausting any exempt amounts), an irrevocable family trust holding the bulk of non-cash-flow assets for multi-generational purposes, liquid cash-flow assets held in personal names with a clear will, and possibly a PPLI wrapper if US-person heirs are involved. Critical — this is specialist territory. I'd bring in SG trust counsel plus any relevant jurisdictional tax counsel early, not late. My role is framing, coordinating, and maintaining the client relationship; the legal work is outsourced. I'd also strongly recommend starting the family-governance conversation in parallel — a family charter draft, a family council, a next-gen education plan. Structures without governance produce fights; governance without structures produces well-intentioned vagueness. Both together is the goal.

### Q2. "What's the difference between a revocable and irrevocable trust, and when would you use each?"

**Answer.** A revocable trust the settlor can amend, modify, or cancel at will. Legally, the settlor is still considered the owner for most tax and liability purposes — so it offers very limited asset protection and no tax separation. Its main purpose is probate avoidance and privacy: assets in the trust bypass the probate process on death, which can save time and court costs and keeps the family affairs out of the public record. Use-case: HNW client who wants administrative convenience and privacy but hasn't decided or isn't ready to fully separate assets. An irrevocable trust the settlor cannot unilaterally amend — typically beneficiary consent plus a formal deed-variation process is required. Legally, the assets are now the trust's, not the settlor's, which gives three benefits: asset protection (from later creditors, divorce, lawsuit); tax separation (the trust has its own tax identity); and true multi-generational continuity (the structure persists beyond the settlor). Use-case: UHNW client who has completed wealth-accumulation and is ready to commit to a permanent structure for next-generation transfer, typically after gift-tax consequences have been pre-planned. In my experience, most UHNW clients start with a revocable arrangement and eventually graduate to irrevocable once life-stage and tax timing align; some jump straight to irrevocable if there's a pressing asset-protection concern or an imminent tax-planning window.

### Q3. "Explain PPLI to a client in 60 seconds."

**Answer.** "PPLI is a private-placement life insurance contract that doubles as a wealth-structuring wrapper. You pay a substantial premium — usually US$2–5 million or more — into a life insurance policy, and the premium funds an investment account held inside the policy, managed per a customised mandate. The investments grow inside the policy without being taxed on income or gains — the growth compounds pre-tax. When you pass away, the beneficiary receives the full value as an insurance death benefit, which in most relevant jurisdictions is not subject to income tax and often bypasses estate tax. So the structure gives you three things: tax deferral on investment growth, clean transfer to beneficiaries, and a level of creditor protection depending on jurisdiction. The costs are real — you're typically paying 100–200 bps a year on top of the investment management fee, and there are surrender penalties if you exit early. And it's not for everyone — if you need access to the funds during your life or if the premium is under about 2 million, the economics don't work. For clients with cross-border heirs, multi-generational goals, and long-horizon capital, it's often the most tax-efficient structure available."

### Q4. "Your client is SG-resident but one of his three children lives in the US. What changes?"

**Answer.** A lot. The US-person child triggers US tax and compliance reach on whatever they inherit or become beneficiary of. Four specific considerations. First, if the family structure is a non-US trust, and the US-person child is a beneficiary, the trust is subject to US FATCA reporting and the child faces throwback-rule taxation on accumulated income — potentially at punitive rates. Second, if the trust holds non-US mutual funds or non-US investment companies, the US-person child is exposed to PFIC (Passive Foreign Investment Company) rules, which can generate surprise taxation on ostensibly normal investments. Third, if the child inherits US-situs assets via the trust or directly, US estate tax and gift-tax considerations kick in at the transfer. Fourth, the child's FBAR and Form 8938 disclosure obligations cover any foreign account or trust interest they have — compliance cost is material and ongoing. Mitigations I'd explore with specialist counsel: structuring the US-person child's share into a US-compliant PPLI or a specifically US-designed trust structure, separating their inheritance stream from the non-US parts of the family wealth, potentially pre-gifting under US annual-exclusion or lifetime-exemption rules while the child's basis is still low. This is not work the bank's counsel can do alone — we'd bring in US tax counsel early and the family should expect an ongoing compliance relationship with US specialists.

### Q5. "When should a client move from bank-PB servicing to a family-office structure?"

**Answer.** Signals, not a fixed threshold. The commonly-cited US$100M is approximately where full Single-Family-Office (SFO) economics make sense — below that, the running costs of 2–5 million a year eat proportionally too much return. But the real signals are qualitative. One, complexity exceeds what a single RM and IC pair can coordinate — multiple jurisdictions, multiple businesses, multi-generational structures, direct private investments, philanthropic operations. Two, the family needs dedicated in-house professionals who put family interest first without banking product bias — CIO, CFO, CAO, legal. Three, direct investing appetite — co-invest deals, private equity done directly rather than via fund, real estate done directly; these benefit from in-house diligence capability. Four, multi-generational governance — requires a professional structure (family council, charter, mentoring) that the family can sustain beyond any individual RM. For US$10M–100M, a Multi-Family Office (MFO) is often the right intermediate step — shared infrastructure, dedicated attention, lower cost than SFO. For SG specifically, the 13O / 13U regime makes the family-office option particularly attractive from about US$50M because of the tax efficiency. Regardless of which route, the transition needs 12–18 months of planning; don't recommend it as a rush decision.
