# Behavioral Finance — The Client Psychology Layer

> **PB is often described as 20% portfolio, 80% psychology.** The interviewer doesn't expect you to be a psychologist, but they expect you to know the common cognitive biases, how they manifest in real clients, and — most importantly — how to structure conversations and decisions to protect clients from themselves. Finance majors often know the academic list (loss aversion, anchoring, recency) but haven't connected each bias to specific RM / IC behaviours. This page closes that loop.

## 直覺理解

Behavioral finance (行為金融學) 本質上是**對古典金融「rational investor」假設的修正**——真實的人在錢面前會做非理性的事，而且這些錯誤是**可預測、有系統、可模式化的**。Daniel Kahneman 的 *Thinking, Fast and Slow* 是這個領域的聖經：System 1 (快、直覺、情緒) 和 System 2 (慢、理性、費力) 兩個思維系統常常打架，System 1 通常贏。對 PB 而言，這意味著：**最常讓客戶損失的不是市場，是客戶自己在市場下跌時的決定**。你作為 RM/IC 的工作不僅是挑產品，是**在客戶情緒戰勝他們之前，把系統性紀律的東西先綁在牆上**（IPS 就是這個牆）。這章介紹最常見 5–7 個 biases、它們在 PB 情境下怎麼出現、以及你怎麼提前架好防線。

## Why this matters for PB specifically

| Without BF awareness | With BF awareness |
|---|---|
| RM pitches products; client emotion drives buy-sell; RM reacts to client moods | RM designs conversations to catch biases early; IPS becomes the shared anchor |
| Client panics in drawdowns, sells bottom, re-buys top | Pre-committed framework; behavioural coaching in the moment |
| Fees and returns are the whole conversation | Psychology and framing add substantial non-return value |
| RM blamed when things go wrong | RM and client share documented responsibility |

**Three academic names to drop comfortably:**

- **Daniel Kahneman** — *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011); Nobel 2002 (with Amos Tversky, posthumously); Prospect Theory.
- **Richard Thaler** — Nobel 2017; *Nudge*; behavioural economics as applied discipline.
- **Amos Tversky** — Kahneman's co-author; died 1996; foundational work on heuristics and biases.

Reading one chapter of *Thinking, Fast and Slow* before an IC interview is time well spent. Your answers get deeper, faster.

## The biases — 7 you must know cold

### 1. Loss Aversion (損失規避)

**The bias:** losses are felt approximately **2x more painfully** than equivalent gains give pleasure (Kahneman-Tversky Prospect Theory). Losing US$10,000 hurts more than winning US$10,000 feels good.

**How it shows up in PB clients:**

- Client refuses to sell a losing position even when the thesis is broken — "I'll hold until it comes back" (the "break-even" fallacy).
- Client sells winners early to "lock in gains" while letting losers run (disposition effect, a direct consequence).
- Client over-weights the pain of a 20% drawdown relative to the joy of a 20% gain — making "balanced" portfolios feel too risky.
- Client takes outsized risks to **avoid** a loss (doubling down after a drawdown) — the "getting back to even" trap.

**RM / IC toolkit:**

- **IPS as pre-committed framework** — "we agreed to this drawdown profile in writing; reverting now crystallises exactly what we anticipated."
- **Framing gains as losses from a higher reference point** — "your portfolio is up 15% YTD; what you're describing as 'losing 5%' is 'locking in 10% gains after giving back 5%'."
- **Pre-commitment to rebalancing** — rebalancing rules force selling winners and buying losers; remove the decision from the emotional moment.
- **Separate "loss" accounts from "growth" accounts** — goals-based bucketing reduces the sting of drawdown on the overall picture.

### 2. Recency Bias (近因偏誤)

**The bias:** we over-weight recent events relative to their statistical importance. The last 6 months feels like "the new normal"; we extrapolate from it.

**How it shows up:**

- After 2022 rate spikes, clients convinced "rates will stay high forever" and loaded on floating-rate everything; missed the 2023-2025 duration rally.
- After a strong bull year, clients want to increase equity allocation above their risk profile.
- Post-CS (March 2023), clients for months refused any perps / AT1 regardless of issuer quality.
- After accumulator losses in 2008, clients for years refused anything with a knock-in barrier.

**RM / IC toolkit:**

- **Show longer-period data** — 20-year returns, multi-cycle comparisons, not YTD.
- **Reference the SAA explicitly** — "SAA was designed across cycles; we don't adjust because one cycle feels loud."
- **Use the last episode's opposite** — after a bear year, remind them of 2013 or 2019; after a bull year, remind them of 2022.
- **Label the bias out loud** — sometimes naming it for the client helps: "this feels like recency reasoning; let's check if the longer pattern supports it."

### 3. Anchoring (錨定偏誤)

**The bias:** we rely too heavily on the first piece of information we encounter (the "anchor") when making subsequent judgements.

**How it shows up:**

- Client fixates on the price they bought a stock at, treating "getting back to breakeven" as the decision frame rather than forward-looking analysis.
- Client benchmarks returns against the peak (e.g. "my portfolio was 12M in 2021 — I've lost 2M"), not the starting capital or long-run target.
- First CIO number heard (e.g. "Fed will cut 3 times") anchors expectations even when data changes.
- Initial fee quote anchors subsequent negotiation.

**RM / IC toolkit:**

- **Forward-only framing in reviews** — "ignore entry price; what's the right action forward from today?"
- **Re-anchor to goals** — "the question isn't 12M-vs-10M peak; it's 10M-vs-your-lifetime-plan."
- **Use benchmark + risk-adjusted framing** — absolute drawdown looks bad; relative-to-benchmark and risk-adjusted can tell different story.
- **Explicitly challenge anchors** — "we're anchoring on the 2021 peak; let's take that off the page for this conversation."

### 4. Confirmation Bias (確認偏誤)

**The bias:** we seek out information that confirms what we already believe and filter out information that contradicts it.

**How it shows up:**

- Client bullish on a name reads only bullish research; dismisses bearish analysts as wrong.
- Client pessimistic on China reads only negative news; misses genuine signals of improvement.
- Client loves a specific fund manager / sector / thesis and over-allocates because supporting evidence is easy to find.
- RM with a view — "rates will drop" — notices only rate-cutting news, misses sticky-inflation signals.

**RM / IC toolkit:**

- **Pre-mortem on positions** — "what would have to happen for this trade to be a mistake? Write it down. Monitor for those signals."
- **Steelman the opposing view** — force yourself to articulate the best case for the contrary.
- **Diverse information diet** — ensure exposure to CIO of competing views, not just own firm.
- **Devil's-advocate assignment** — in team / RM-IC review, one person argues the contra position explicitly.

### 5. Overconfidence Bias (過度自信)

**The bias:** we overestimate our ability to predict, our knowledge, and our judgement accuracy. Research consistently shows most people rate themselves as "above average" on investing acumen — mathematically impossible.

**How it shows up:**

- Client (often a successful business person) believes their business judgement transfers to investing; concentrated bets that go wrong.
- Client resists SAA discipline because "I've got a feel for markets."
- Newly-liquid client (post-exit) convinced they'll beat the market through their own thinking.
- RM / IC certain of a call that doesn't play out.

**RM / IC toolkit:**

- **Humility-framed analytics** — show historical hit rate of similar "high conviction" calls; most are 55–60% right.
- **Size over direction** — "if you're right, how big the win; if wrong, how big the loss; size accordingly."
- **Reserve conviction for high-information situations** — client who genuinely knows their own sector may have legitimate edge; client who reads Bloomberg does not.
- **Use "I could be wrong" in your own speech** — models humility.

### 6. Herding / Social Proof (從眾效應)

**The bias:** we follow what others are doing because their collective action feels like information.

**How it shows up:**

- Client piles into the "hot" thematic of the quarter (AI 2023-24, crypto 2021, China 2017, tech 1999).
- Client refuses an under-owned position even when the thesis is sound, because "no one else is buying."
- Fund-flow chasing — the hottest fund category in any year is typically late in its cycle.
- RM recommendations drift toward what the peer desk is pushing.

**RM / IC toolkit:**

- **Explicit flow-vs-fundamentals distinction** — "here's what the crowd is doing; here's what the fundamentals support; these differ."
- **Delayed execution for momentum ideas** — "if you still want this in 30 days after the hype quiets, we'll do it."
- **Size constraints on thematic** — max 10% thematic even when the theme looks compelling.
- **Historical base-rate on thematic sells** — show client the history of thematic ETFs that peaked at inflows.

### 7. Mental Accounting (心理帳戶)

**The bias:** we treat money differently based on its source or label, despite money being fungible.

**How it shows up:**

- Client treats bonus money as "fun money" for speculation while being conservative with "base salary" money.
- "Gift money" or "inherited money" spent more impulsively than earned money.
- Clients create sub-portfolios (retirement, college, growth) and apply inconsistent risk to each despite holistic view being more appropriate (or more appropriate — goals-based is legitimate; mental-accounting for arbitrary reasons isn't).
- "Already-made profits" treated as playing-with-house-money for outsized risk.

**RM / IC toolkit:**

- **Goals-based structuring** — intentional mental accounting that aligns with genuine life-goals is healthy; arbitrary labelling isn't.
- **Full-portfolio view in every review** — don't let sub-accounts evade scrutiny.
- **Risk is total-portfolio, not per-bucket** — coach clients that concentration in one bucket is still concentration.

### Bonus — Status Quo Bias (現狀偏誤)

**The bias:** preferring things to stay as they are; defaulting to inaction.

**How it shows up:**

- Client keeps legacy positions (inherited, employer-stock, old bonds) long past when they'd buy them fresh.
- Resists rebalancing because "things are fine."
- Keeps underperforming fund manager because switching feels active and scary.

**RM / IC toolkit:**

- **"Would you buy this today at current prices?" test** — if no, it's a sell.
- **Scheduled reviews force action** — quarterly rebalancing as agenda item breaks inertia.

## System 1 vs System 2 — the Kahneman frame

**System 1** — fast, intuitive, automatic, emotional, pattern-matching. Handles 95% of daily cognition. Great at familiar decisions; terrible at novel / statistical / low-probability ones.

**System 2** — slow, deliberate, analytical, effortful. Handles complex reasoning. Tires easily (decision fatigue); often lazy (will defer to System 1's snap judgement).

**Implications for PB conversations:**

- **Market-stress calls** fire System 1 ("sell everything!"); good RM structure forces System 2 engagement (appointment for tomorrow, run the numbers, reference the IPS).
- **"I need a decision today"** pressure is usually a System 1 tell; resist.
- **Simple framing beats complex** — clients can engage System 2 on simple quantitative comparisons, but complex multivariate framings lose them.
- **Pre-commitment mechanisms** (IPS, rebalancing rules, gate-keeping on large trades) are System 2 safeguards installed before System 1 panic.

## How good RMs / ICs actually apply this

### 1. Design the conversation architecture up-front

- IPS with explicit drawdown scenarios and pre-committed actions.
- "What would you do if your portfolio dropped 30%?" is a conversation worth having in calm markets.
- Documented risk-tolerance that triangulates stated + behavioural + capacity + psychology (see [`wealth_management.md`](wealth_management.md)).

### 2. Intervene at the decision moment

- Demand in-person / video for crisis conversations — no phone-only panic sells.
- Reference written pre-commitments.
- Run the math visibly with the client.
- Offer small compromises that reduce anxiety without derailing the plan.

### 3. Document and label biases explicitly

- In reviews: "this looks like recency bias from last quarter's events — let's check a longer view."
- Normalising bias-language reduces its power.

### 4. Structure rebalancing to remove emotion from execution

- Calendar-based or threshold-based, committed to in writing.
- Automated where possible.

### 5. Bias-awareness in your own decisions

- ICs should have a pre-trade checklist: what's the bias risk on this view?
- RMs should self-audit: am I pushing a product because of confirmation bias in my own reading?

## Interview questions

### Q1. "A client calls wanting to sell everything after a 25% drawdown. Name the biases at play and how you'd handle the conversation."

**Answer.** Three biases operating simultaneously. First, loss aversion — the 25% drawdown is being experienced at roughly 2x the intensity of an equivalent gain; System 1 is firing hard. Second, recency bias — the last few weeks of bad news feels like the new normal; the client is extrapolating. Third, anchoring — they're anchored on the peak portfolio value, not the long-run plan or starting capital. How I'd handle: first, don't take the decision by phone. Request a same-day meeting, face-to-face or video. This forces the client's System 2 to engage through time-delay and structured conversation. Second, listen fully before responding — the fear needs to be aired, not dismissed. Third, re-ground in the IPS we agreed: we anticipated drawdowns of this magnitude, the pre-committed response was not to sell. Fourth, run the math visibly: expected 6-month recovery probability based on historical comparable episodes; realised loss if we sell now versus held through; the "getting back to even" fallacy numerically. Fifth, offer a small action that reduces anxiety without derailing — maybe 5% of equity moved to T-bills as psychological buffer. Sixth, commit to daily check-ins for the next two weeks. The goal isn't to lecture about biases — it's to structurally re-engage System 2 while validating the emotion.

### Q2. "Tell me about Prospect Theory and how it affects how you recommend structured products."

**Answer.** Prospect Theory, from Kahneman and Tversky 1979, has two key findings relevant here. First, loss aversion — losses hurt about twice as much as equivalent gains feel good. Second, probability distortion — people overweight small probabilities (why lotteries sell) and underweight moderate probabilities. For structured products, these combine badly. A yield-enhancement product like a DCI or ELN is attractive because the headline coupon (say 8% vs 4% bank deposit) looks like a certain gain. The knock-in / conversion scenario is framed as low-probability. Client's System 1 under-weights that low probability and over-weights the certain coupon gain. When the knock-in actually triggers, loss aversion hits — the pain of the realised loss is 2x the pleasure of the received coupons. Net behavioural outcome: clients over-buy these structures in calm markets, then feel burned when they trigger, and refuse all structured products for years afterward — which is also excessive. My approach: frame the product honestly as "selling an insurance-like payoff for a premium" not "earning a high coupon"; ensure the client can articulate the tail scenario back in their own words; size the position so the tail scenario wouldn't be catastrophic; and specifically calibrate expectations — "if the tail hits, you'll feel 2x worse than the math shows; pre-commit that we hold through the tail rather than panic-exit." This is behavioural scaffolding around a legitimate product.

### Q3. "How do you recognise when a client is affected by recency bias?"

**Answer.** Several tells. One — they extrapolate a narrow time window. "Rates have been rising all year, so they'll keep rising" or "this stock is up 30% this quarter, so next quarter will be similar." Two — they use "now" or "these days" rather than cycle-or-base-rate framing. "Now markets are volatile," "these days nothing is safe." Three — they treat the last move as evidence without reference to longer history. "AT1 blew up — they're all risky" (post-CS) or "tech is unbeatable" (2020-21). Four — they adjust allocations based on the last quarter's performance, wanting more of what did well recently. When I see these, I don't directly correct — I introduce longer-period data gently. "Yes, the last six months have been volatile; let's look at how your portfolio would have handled comparable periods in 2018, 2022, 2008." Then the longer pattern becomes the shared frame. I also use it on myself — when I find myself extrapolating recent moves into conviction trades, I force myself to look at the 5-year or longer base rate.

### Q4. "What behavioural mechanisms can you build into an IPS?"

**Answer.** Several, and the good IPS does this deliberately. First, pre-committed rebalancing rules — calendar or threshold-based — that remove emotion from the sell-winners / buy-losers decision in volatile times. Second, explicit drawdown scenarios with pre-committed responses — "if portfolio drops 20%, we will not sell equity; we may rebalance from bonds to equity if the allocation has drifted." Third, satellite-sleeve constraints — if the client has the urge to make tactical bets, cap the satellite at 10-15% of portfolio so even if those bets go badly, the core is preserved. Fourth, goals-based bucketing with explicit drawdown tolerance per bucket — the "lifestyle reserve" has zero drawdown tolerance and sits in T-bills; the "legacy bucket" has 40% drawdown tolerance and can be equity-heavy; separating mental accounts structurally beats letting the client improvise under stress. Fifth, review-cadence rules — quarterly formal reviews force discussion, annual deep reviews force re-examination. Sixth, decision-rights rules — trades above a threshold notional require 48-hour cooling-off period; this delays System-1 snap decisions. Each of these is a pre-committed structure that the client agrees to in a calm moment and can be referenced in the stressed moment.

### Q5. "What's one bias you recognise in yourself that you actively manage?"

**Answer** (personalise with genuine answer): Template — confirmation bias. When I form a market view I notice myself reading the analysts who agree with me and dismissing those who don't. The counter-discipline I've developed: before finalising any conviction trade, I write down the three best reasons it could be wrong, and check whether any of the three would actually make me update my view or whether I'd rationalise them away. If I catch myself rationalising, that's a signal my conviction is weaker than I think. A second bias I watch is overconfidence — after a few calls have played out well, I notice the temptation to size bigger; I use a fixed sizing discipline (X% of satellite per conviction trade regardless of confidence) to stop that drift. Both are admissions I make openly with clients because it signals disciplined process over hero-narrative.
